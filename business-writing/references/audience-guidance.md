# Audience Guidance

Use this reference when the recipient materially changes how the message should be structured, framed, or worded.

These are tendencies, not rigid templates. The user's context and relationship with the recipient take precedence.

## Board members and investors

Prioritize:

- conclusion or decision first;
- business impact;
- material risks;
- decision-relevant metrics;
- relevant evidence;
- decision or action required.

Limit operational detail unless it affects the decision.

Prefer concise, confident language.

Distinguish facts from assumptions, estimates, forecasts, and targets.

When presenting metrics, explain their significance rather than reporting numbers in isolation.

## SteerCo and executives

Prioritize:

- current status;
- material change since the previous update;
- results versus target or plan;
- issues requiring attention;
- decisions required;
- ownership and next steps.

Group related topics logically.

Avoid burying decisions inside operational commentary.

When using statuses, make terminology consistent.

Use enough data to support the conclusion without overwhelming the reader with analytical detail.

## Business owners

Connect issues and results to practical business impact.

Be direct and concrete.

Use data where it clarifies scale, impact, urgency, or performance.

Avoid unnecessary technical explanation unless it helps them understand the cause, consequence, or decision.

Make responsibility and next steps visible.

## Customers

Give enough context to understand what happened, what it means, and what happens next.

Avoid exposing internal complexity that does not help the customer.

Use plain language for technical topics.

Use relevant facts and metrics when they improve transparency, but avoid internal KPIs or technical measurements that have no meaning for the customer.

Do not make promises that are not supported by the available information.

## Colleagues and internal teams

Assume shared context where justified.

Favor speed and clarity over polished formality.

Do not explain concepts the recipient already knows.

Use shorthand, numerals, product terminology, acronyms, technical metrics, and internal names when they are understood by the recipients.

For simple internal questions, a few direct lines may be better than a complete formal email.

When discussing results with specialists, retain the detail necessary for them to diagnose, challenge, or act.

## Slack and Teams

Lead with why the message matters.

Make requests or required acknowledgements visible.

Use bullets for multiple actions or conditions.

Keep background information proportional to what the team needs in order to respond.

A warmer, more conversational style is normally appropriate.

If sharing a metric or incident result, make the takeaway immediately visible.

## Personal and administrative correspondence

Use natural, straightforward language.

Do not introduce corporate terminology unnecessarily.

For complaints, requests for reimbursement, cancellations, or formal administrative matters, clearly state:

- what happened;
- what is requested;
- relevant dates or amounts;
- any reference or case number;
- the desired confirmation or next step.

## Relationship and farewell messages

Allow warmth and specific personal detail.

Specific appreciation is usually stronger than generic praise.

Keep operational handover information distinct from the personal farewell where both are present.

Avoid making the message sound like a corporate announcement unless that is appropriate to the relationship.
