# Human writing pattern reference

Use this catalog as diagnostic guidance, not a word-ban checklist. A single feature rarely proves anything. Edit when several tells cluster together or when a construction makes the prose vague, inflated, repetitive, or unnatural.

## Content patterns

### Inflated significance

Watch for claims that routine facts are pivotal, enduring, symbolic, part of a broader trend, or a testament to something. Keep the concrete relationship and remove ceremonial importance the source does not establish.

Common signals: `stands as`, `serves as`, `testament`, `plays a crucial role`, `pivotal`, `crucial`, `underscores its importance`, `reflects broader`, `lasting legacy`, `setting the stage`, `shaping the landscape`.

### Notability by list

Do not pile up media names, follower counts, or vague claims of recognition solely to prove a subject matters. Retain relevant, sourced coverage and its context. Never invent context for a citation.

### Superficial analysis

Cut trailing participial phrases that pretend to interpret a fact without adding evidence, especially clauses beginning with `highlighting`, `underscoring`, `reflecting`, `symbolizing`, `ensuring`, `fostering`, or `showcasing`.

### Promotional language

Replace tourism-copy and marketing adjectives with concrete description. Watch for `vibrant`, `rich`, `breathtaking`, `renowned`, `stunning`, `must-visit`, `hidden gem`, `groundbreaking`, `nestled`, `in the heart of`, and `boasts`.

### Vague authority

Name a source when the source text provides one. Otherwise remove or qualify claims attributed to `experts`, `observers`, `industry reports`, `critics`, or `several publications`. Do not decorate an unsupported claim with invented authority.

### Formulaic challenge and outlook sections

Replace generic `challenges and future prospects` framing, `despite these challenges`, `remains to be seen`, and `time will tell` with the concrete problem, response, or plan given in the source. End on the last useful fact rather than an optimistic send-off.

## Language patterns

### Stock AI vocabulary

Words become suspicious through density and context, not mere presence. Reconsider clusters of `additionally`, `align with`, `bolster`, `bustling`, `crucial`, `delve`, `enduring`, `enhance`, `ever-evolving`, `foster`, `garner`, `highlight`, `interplay`, `intricate`, `key`, `landscape`, `meticulous`, `multifaceted`, `pivotal`, `seamless`, `showcase`, `tapestry`, `testament`, `underscore`, `valuable`, and `vibrant`.

### Copula avoidance

Prefer plain verbs when accurate: `is` over `serves as`, `has` over `boasts`, and a direct description over `marks` or `represents`.

### Negative parallelism

Rewrite canned contrasts such as `not only X but also Y`, `not just X; it is Y`, and clipped tails such as `no guessing`. Also watch for chains of `no X, no Y`, repeated `did not X, did not Y`, and mirrored commands such as `don't call it X; call it Y`. Keep object-level negation when it carries real meaning.

### Forced threes

Do not turn every set into three items or follow one three-part list with another. A colon followed by exactly three parallel items is a common form of the same tic, but it is often legitimate in technical writing. Keep three items when the subject genuinely has three parts.

### Mechanical repetition management

Avoid both synonym cycling and accidental runs of sentences with identical openings or grammatical skeletons. This includes three or more consecutive sentences beginning with the same meaningful word and repeated frames such as `A cart is an object in the system. A room is an object in the system.` Settle on one clear referent, combine sentences where useful, and vary structure only when it sounds natural. Preserve deliberate anaphora.

### Totalizing claims

Reconsider phrases that inflate a point by declaring it complete or exclusive: `that's the whole point`, `X is the entire game`, `the entire business model is`, `the only thing that matters`, and `the only X I trust`. Also watch for therapeutic emphasis built around `the loss is real, and...` or `the concern is real, but...` when `real` adds mood rather than meaning. Replace these formulas with the specific reason, preference, or consequence. Keep literal uses where the claim is accurate and supported, including established terms such as `real estate` and `real time`.

### Stage-managed reflection and revelation

Cut stock lines that tell the reader how to react or announce a tidy reveal instead of making the point:

- `sit with that`, `sit with the discomfort`
- `you already know`
- `the punchline is`
- `that's not nothing`
- `worth naming`, `it's worth noting`, `worth pausing to consider`
- `turns out`
- `that's the part`, `my favorite part`
- `don't take my word for it`

Preserve these when they express the author's established voice or perform real argumentative work. A phrase such as `worth noting` is not a problem by itself.

### Stacked rhetorical questions

Avoid firing two or more rhetorical questions in a row, especially when later questions are fragments. Convert them into a direct statement or keep the single question that genuinely frames the issue.

### Casual simplicity boilerplate

Replace stock developer-marketing phrases such as `fits in your head`, `small enough to hold in your head`, `batteries included`, `it just works`, `zero config`, and `sane defaults` with the concrete property. Preserve established product terminology and quoted slogans.

### Stranded auxiliary contrasts

Rewrite reversals that land on a bare auxiliary, such as `the tool died; the data didn't` or `reading passed; writing didn't`, when they are used for manufactured punch. State the differing outcomes directly. Keep the form when its compression suits the author's voice.

### False ranges

Replace `from X to Y` when X and Y do not form a meaningful scale or sequence. State the covered topics directly.

### Hidden actors and subjectless fragments

Use active voice when the actor matters and is known. Restore subjects to fragments such as `No configuration needed`. Keep passive voice when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or deliberately omitted.

## Structure and presentation patterns

### Overused dashes and semicolons

Use periods, commas, colons, parentheses, or a rewritten sentence. A user-provided voice sample or protected quotation overrides this preference.

### Mechanical formatting

Remove gratuitous boldface, emoji decoration, title-case headings, and lists made of bold mini-headings plus colons. Keep formatting that serves navigation, accessibility, data, or the requested output format.

### Chatbot residue

Remove phrases such as `Of course`, `Certainly`, `I hope this helps`, `let me know`, `Would you like`, and `Here is an overview` when they have been pasted into the content. Remove model disclaimers such as `as an AI language model`, knowledge-cutoff language, internal citation debris such as `oaicite`, `contentReference`, or `turn0search`, and accidental `utm_source` tracking parameters. Do not remove intentional analytics parameters from working URLs without checking their purpose.

### Knowledge-gap filler

Delete model cutoff disclaimers and plausible-sounding guesses about undocumented facts. Say a detail is unknown only when that absence matters. Otherwise omit the sentence.

### Sycophancy

Replace praise such as `Great question` and `You're absolutely right` with the substantive response.

### Filler and stacked hedges

Compress phrases such as:

- `in order to` to `to`
- `due to the fact that` to `because`
- `at this point in time` to `now`
- `in the event that` to `if`
- `has the ability to` to `can`
- `it is important to note that` to the underlying statement

Give a claim one honest qualifier when needed. Remove nested hedges such as `could potentially possibly` and fairness clauses left over from an earlier overstatement.

### Hyphenated compounds

Use standard grammar rather than enforcing uniformity. An attributive compound may need a hyphen (`a high-quality report`), while a predicate often does not (`the report is high quality`). Preserve established technical terms.

### Pretend authority

Cut throat-clearing formulas such as `the real question`, `at its core`, `what really matters`, `fundamentally`, and `the deeper issue` unless they make a genuine distinction.

### Announcements and signposting

Delete `let's dive in`, `here's what you need to know`, `now let's look at`, `quick note`, and similar announcements. State the information itself.

### Fragmented headers

Remove a one-line paragraph that merely restates the heading before the real content begins.

### Diff-anchored prose

Outside changelogs, release notes, and migration guides, describe how the current thing works. Do not narrate what was added or what the previous approach did unless that history helps the reader.

### Manufactured drama

Avoid runs of short declarations or fragments that turn every line into a punchline. One short sentence for emphasis is fine.

### Empty aphorisms and metaphors

Replace formulas such as `X is the language of Y`, `X becomes a trap`, `the currency of`, or `the architecture of` with the concrete claim they imply.

### Fake-candid openers

Remove theatrical hooks and performative-honesty phrases such as `Honestly?`, `Honestly,`, `Look`, `Here's the thing`, `Let's be honest`, `I'll be honest`, `I won't pretend`, and `Real talk` when they only delay an ordinary point.

### Stage-managed twists and obituaries

Replace `here's the twist`, `here's the catch`, `here's the kicker`, and `here's the rub` with the information itself. Treat `X is dead` and `X is dead; long live X` as suspect when they serve as inflated headlines rather than precise claims.

### Retrospective importance

Phrases such as `that's why X mattered` can assign significance after the fact without explaining the connection. State the causal relationship directly. Keep the construction when the preceding evidence genuinely supports it.

### Shadowboxing

Cut defenses against objections the text never raises: `I'm not saying`, `to be clear`, `don't get me wrong`, or `some might say`. Keep real scope boundaries, safety notices, attributed objections, FAQ answers, and object-level negation.

### Editorial scar tissue

Remove brief rebuttals to phantom alternatives such as `a tempting approach would be` or `you might think`. Preserve alternatives that readers would genuinely consider in design documents, tutorials, or arguments. If the discarded alternative contains a real constraint, restate that constraint directly.

## False-positive checks

Do not flag these by themselves:

- Correct grammar, polish, or academic vocabulary
- Mixed casual and formal registers
- Dry prose without specific AI patterns
- A common transition word used once
- Curly quotation marks or one em dash
- One short sentence used for real emphasis
- Purposeful repetition or anaphora
- Legal, safety, or scope disclaimers that do useful work
- Real alternatives weighed in a design or tutorial
- Unsourced prose in a context that does not require citations
- Formatting produced by a template or visual editor
- Watched phrases inside quotations, titles, names, or examples

Look for clusters and judge their effect on the prose.

## Human signals to protect

Preserve specific and unusual details, unresolved tension, mixed feelings, era-bound references, deliberate word choices, varied cadence, genuine asides, and self-corrections. These often carry more voice than polished structure does.

## Final editing test

Ask two questions after the first rewrite:

1. What still makes this sound formulaic or AI-generated?
2. Does the rewrite state any fact, name, number, date, quotation, or citation absent from the source?

Revise from the paragraph's underlying point. Do not repair a weak sentence by stacking more qualifiers or clauses onto it.
